# Brief — the deck, the cast, the affordances

> **Superseded.** This brief produced `CAST.md` and `FACTS.md` (its deliverables
> 1 and 2) and is kept for provenance. Its cast size, its belief model, its
> budget and its deliverable list all predate `HORRORS.md` and `MECHANICS.md`.
> For the situations, write against `situations-brief.md` instead.

You are writing the content bible for a short narrative horror game called
**THE WELL**. You will not write, read, or reason about code, and nothing you
produce will be typed into a compiler by you. Everything below is a fiction with
a small number of hard rules; your job is to fill it with people, situations and
prose.

Work in the vocabulary given in §3. It is not decoration — every term maps to
something the machine actually does, and content written outside the vocabulary
cannot be built.

---

## 1. The premise

At the bottom of an abandoned well, a dead presence watches the living pass
overhead. It cannot leave, cannot speak, and cannot touch the world — except
through the things that once belonged to it, which are down here in the silt with
its body.

The player is that presence. They discover they are dead in the first few
minutes, and are never told so. There is no objective, no inventory, no dialogue,
no puzzle. The player observes, experiments, and slowly learns that they can
change what the living believe happened here.

A run is **5–12 minutes, and the length is not a target — it is a consequence.**
The more frightening the well becomes, the shorter the scenes get and the sooner
the living stop giving the presence anything to watch, so a quiet run runs long
and a violent one ends early. The game is built to be replayed. A player sees
roughly half the deck in any one run.

Two levers, and only two, for the whole game:

- **Press** — haunting. Noticeable wrongness: the water, the sound, the cold, the
  light. Spends *presence*, which recovers only by being still.
- **Hold** — resonance. Taking up one of the belongings and letting what it means
  bleed into whoever is above. Spends that belonging's charge, which **never
  comes back**.

They are mutually exclusive moment to moment. You cannot press and hold at once,
so the interesting play is *timing inside a scene*: hold for two beats, press on
the last one.

**Nobody living can perceive the presence.** That constraint is absolute and it is
what the whole game is made of. The one fiction-legal exemption is animals.

The one canon event: at some point, mid-to-late, **a living person is thrown into
the well.** The player cannot stop it by choosing to; they can only have made a
village in which it goes differently.

## 2. What already exists

### The people

Four in play, one held back.

| who | what they are | carrying |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Anna** | has drawn water from this well for a year without knowing why she hates it | grief, a little curiosity |
| **Tomas** | whatever happened, he was there for the end of it | guilt, some fear |
| **Anselm** | the old man who decides what the village is allowed to say out loud | a little fear |
| **the Ferrin boy** | a child, and the only one who wants the well to be interesting | curiosity |
| **the stranger** | not in play until the throwing | — |

The emotions a person can carry, and there are only these six: **grief, fear,
guilt, curiosity, anger, tenderness.**

What the village can decide the well *is*, and there are only these four:
**haunted, mystery, tragedy, danger.**

### The belongings

Four, each one a piece of the death told sideways.

- **a brass ring**, green at the edges, on a finger, on a hand in the silt —
  tenderness. Anna's, unmistakably.
- **a tin whistle**, flattened where a boot came down — curiosity. The boy's, or
  meant for a child who never got it.
- **a short knife**, bedded to the hilt as though it went in after you did —
  guilt. Tomas goes quiet near it.
- **a coat**, spread and heavy with water, torn at the shoulder in a way the fall
  does not explain — grief. Everyone feels this one a little.

### The dog

One plot is already written to full depth, and it is the tutorial. Three
situations, six outcomes, three facts. A dog comes to the rim; a dog can perceive
the presence and people cannot; whatever the player does to it, the owner comes
looking, and what the owner finds is caused by the player while *when* they come
is a die roll. It exists to teach, on something disposable: **player → dog →
what people make of the dog → what the village starts to believe.**

Its tone warning generalises to everything you write: *a dog is one warm
adjective from wrecking the register.* Strange means **wrong**, never funny. It
stops barking. It lies down at the rim. It looks directly at the player and does
not stop.

## 3. The vocabulary you must write in

**Situation.** A thing that *can* happen, with its conditions attached — not a
thing that happens. "Someone comes for water at dusk" is a situation. Who comes
is decided when it is dealt, not when it is written.

**Roles and cast.** A situation declares slots — `the-one-who-comes`, `the-other`,
`a witness (optional)` — with requirements: tags, emotional range, what they must
or must not already know. The machine fills them at deal time. An optional role is
the cheapest variation in the design: the same situation with a witness and
without one is two scenes.

**Gates are bands, with a floor *and a ceiling*.** Nothing is ever gated only on a
minimum. `two women talking freely at the rim: attention 0.0–0.35` — once the
village is watching, nobody is careless near this well again, and that content is
gone. Write the ceiling first. If you cannot name what stops being true, you have
not thought about the situation yet.

**Facts** are the connective tissue: small named truths — `dog.loose`,
`body.found`, `she.has-stopped-coming`, `unguarded`. Situations declare everything
they need as facts and **never name another situation.** If a line of prose wants
to say *as she said before*, that is a fact that has not been written yet.

**Registers.** How a situation is played: `plain, tender, wary, careless, formal,
raw`. Not paint, and **not something most situations have.** A register is a *tell
on the main plot* — when a scene plays tender, or raw, the player is being told
something about the story underneath, and that is the only reason to write one.
Most situations have one implicit register and no variation. Reserve them for the
five or so that carry plot weight: a register on a throwaway scene is a false
signal, which is worse than no signal at all.

The rule that makes fear mechanical: **the soft registers (tender, careless, raw)
leave the deck above dread ≈ 0.5, unless someone in the cast is `unguarded`** —
drunk, a child, too old to care, or believing they are alone. That last one is the
good one, because *believing you are alone* is something the player can destroy.

Because the registers are the tells, this rule has a sharp consequence worth
writing toward: **a player who frightens the village loses the ability to read its
story.** Tenderness does not disappear from the game, it becomes rare and
therefore devastating — and so does information.

**Recurrence.** Some content is reachable *only by repetition* — not by a stat,
not by a flag, only by the fourth time. Anna comes for water and comes for water
and comes for water, and on the fourth visit the text erodes into something no
gate could have unlocked.

Two disciplines. It belongs to **a handful of events, not to the deck at large** —
most situations are written once and never erode. And it is counted **against the
person, not the situation**: "the fourth time" almost always means the fourth time
*with her*, across whatever situations she has been cast in, rather than the
fourth dealing of one scene. Write recurrence as character arcs. Write the text as
variants of the same situation, never as new situations.

**Outcomes score; they do not match.** Every outcome offers a number and the
highest wins, so a scene can come out grief-*and*-fear rather than whichever
condition was listed first. Write compound outcomes that beat both singles when
both inputs are present.

**Affordances.** What *pressing* is in this particular scene, and what *holding*
is. Same two levers, same two pools, different fiction and different magnitude:
the water goes wrong at the rim, the lamp gutters at the hearing, the dog's ears
go flat. Two laws — **affordances are never listed to the player**, they are found
by trying; and **every affordance prints prose in the beat it lands**, always,
because silent input is what makes players spam.

**Cues.** Named sensory beats attached to outcomes — `water-wrong`, `stone-tick`.
Just name them; they cost nothing now and cannot be retrofitted later.

**Threads.** A chain of situations whose internal order is guaranteed while
everything else is dealt at random, running on its own clock so it advances
whether or not the player is watching. A missed step usually resolves **silently**
— the facts land, nobody narrates it, and the player meets the consequence later
without having seen the cause.

**`Below` — the three tiers.** Everything narrated from the bottom of the well is
rewritten by the presence's growing self-knowledge, in three named tiers:

```
veiled   sensation. no names, no certainty.
         the small hard thing. cold, and it will not warm.
plain    the thing named, its history not yet claimed.
         a ring. it does not fit anything down here.
named    yours. said so.
         your ring. she gave it back and you carried it in your pocket ever since.
extra    only at `named` — an additional beat that exists at no other tier
```

There is **more text** at higher tiers, not just different text, so awareness is
felt as the world getting louder rather than clearer. The belongings run one tier
ahead of the walls and the water. The word *dead* is never required; the narration
simply stops being evasive.

**This applies only to Below.** Up There is observed, not remembered, and its
prose must not know anything the presence has not earned.

### The readout law

There are meters underneath, and the player must never see one. Each has exactly
one channel in the prose:

| meter | channel |
| --- | --- |
| attention | how often the light above is interrupted; passer-by versus player-targeted situations; whether people linger |
| dread | scenes are cut short; registers and situations leave the deck; the endings that want an early exit open |
| lucidity | Below prose names things; more text; how explicit the mechanics are allowed to be |
| charge | the water's behaviour; whether the belongings are warm to hold |
| beliefs | **overheard dialogue only** — the verdict is something you hear people reach |

Be strictest about the last one. **There is never a summary of what the village
thinks.** The player finds out what the well has become by listening to two people
disagree about it.

## 4. The budget

This is arithmetic, not taste, and it constrains what you write.

- **15–20 situations for the whole game.** Not sixty. Sixty flat scenes is a deck
  the player can map; twenty situations × cast × the occasional register is one
  they cannot. Depth, not breadth — this is the single most important instruction
  here.
- **A run is 10–12 situation instances played quietly, and 7–9 played hard**, from
  a deck of twenty. The player sees about half the deck and meets the other half
  on the next run. That gap is the whole reason the deck is worth building.
- **Dread cuts scenes short**, and what it takes away it pays for by opening the
  situations that invite an early ending. It is *not* a prose variant and you
  should never write one for it: the same situation at high dread simply stops
  early, because people do not finish what they came to say when saying it here
  has become a thing one does not do. Every situation declares where it can be cut
  and the one line that ends it there. That line is the whole of dread's prose cost.
- **Dread never recovers.** Every scene the player cuts short stays cut short for
  the rest of the run; the run gets faster and thinner as it goes and there is no
  way back. A situation may grant small relief. Nothing grants much.
- **Four situations are "barometers"** — deliberately recurring, deliberately
  written to carry the full range from warm to closed-up, and to still read when
  cut from four beats to one. They are how the player takes the temperature of the
  village, because they are the scenes they see repeatedly. Nominate yours, and
  write them so they are plausible at every temperature from the first draft.
- Rough shape of a situation: **4 beats, one register, 3 outcomes, no recurrence.**
  The plot-bearing few with registers and the handful with character recurrence are
  exceptions, and you should be able to say why each one earns it.
- **~19,000 words total across the project**, of which the coda is a quarter.
  Budget accordingly and do not write a novel for situation three.

## 5. What to deliver, in this order

**Stop after item 1 and item 2 and show me those before writing any situations.**
Everything downstream is written against that vocabulary and it is expensive to
change afterwards.

### 1. The cast pool — the most urgent item

There are four people in play and the casting system needs **nine to twelve**, or
"prefer the person we have seen least" is a rotation rather than a choice.

For each: name, one line of who they are, **tags** (`child`, `old`, `devout`,
`drinks`, `outsider`, `animal`, …), starting emotions in words rather than
numbers, which belongings they have an affinity with and why, and — the part that
matters most — **what they are mechanically for**: which roles they can fill that
nobody else can, and which situations become possible because they exist.

Plus a relation map: `kin`, `married`, `promised`, `estranged`, `strangers`. Small
and specific. A village of nine people with four relationships between them is a
village; nine strangers is a list.

Keep the five existing people and build around them. At least one person who is
`unguarded` by disposition, and at least one whose being unguarded is a fact the
player can take away.

### 2. The fact dictionary

25–40 facts, grouped by subject — the well, the body, the village, each person.
For each: the name, one line of what being true means, and **at least two
different situations that could plausibly establish it.** A fact with one origin
is hostage to one deal; a fact with three is a piece of world that will be true
eventually.

Include the ambient ones — the facts that are not plot, that several unrelated
situations read as weather.

### 3. Fifteen to twenty situation sheets

Format in §6. Cover the whole attention range: intimate scenes with low ceilings,
the middle where people come to check a rumour, the crowded end where the village
arrives as a body. Include the throwing and whatever leads to it, and at least one
thread of three steps that runs without the player.

### 4. Three of them written to full depth

You choose which; say why. **Pick expensive ones** — at least one carrying
registers and one carrying character recurrence — because the point of the sample
is to price the corpus, and pricing it off three cheap situations will tell you it
is half the size it is. Every register they have, every recurrence level, the
cut-short line, all prose actually written rather than described. This should be
the quality you intend, not a sketch.

### 5. The belongings, deepened

For each of the four: its three `Below` tiers plus the `extra` beat, and its
**second face** — what it meant to *someone still alive*, which is a different
thing from what it meant to the presence and unlocks by seeing that person near
the well enough times.

Then the rest of `Below`: the water, the walls, the silt, the rope, the light, the
cold, the body, the sky, and whatever else down there is worth naming. Aim for
about eighteen subjects in total, three tiers each.

### 6. The overheard channel

The dialogue through which beliefs are the only way they may ever be expressed.
Write pairs of people at the rim disagreeing about what the well is, keyed to
which belief is winning and by how much. This is a small system doing a large job
and it needs its own vocabulary of half-sentences and things people decline to
say.

### 7. The coda — and it is a quarter of the project

**20–30 variants**, across *what the village decided* × *how closed up it got* ×
the few facts that change an ending outright. About **5,000 words**, and after the
beats themselves it is the largest single thing you will write.

That weight is deliberate. This game hides its state on purpose — beliefs are
overheard, never summarised — so unlike a game with a visible score, the player
has spent the whole run accumulating something they were never allowed to watch.
The coda is where they find out. It is the one place permitted to say
retroactively what the well became, it is the most-read content in a game people
replay, and it is the thing two players will compare.

**The short, frightened run needs the strongest coda of all.** A player who
haunted hard gets the run that ends earliest and tells them least: four minutes,
and a village that stopped talking. Without a coda that lands *why*, that reads as
the game breaking rather than as the thing they did. Write those first and best —
the well nobody will stand near, the story nobody finished telling, the silence
the player made and now has to sit inside.

## 6. The situation sheet format

```
## <name> — <one line of what it is>

gate         attention 0.30–0.75 · dread 0.0–0.60
             facts all [...] none [...]
roles        <role> (tags, what they must already be) · <role> (optional)
distinct     [...]
registers    [wary, plain]   or  —  (most situations)
recurrence   against <person> · cooldown ~6 · escalates / does not   or  —
cut short    after beat 2 · <the one line that ends it there>
barometer    yes / no
thread       <thread name, step n> or —

beats
  1  <what is heard. sound before sight, always>
  2  <what is seen through the coin of sky>
  3  <the turn>
  4  <the moment it could still go either way>

affords
  press  'the water goes wrong'  → <what the people up there do about it>
  hold   'it goes cold'          → <what changes in them>

outcomes
  <name>    scores highest when …
            sets     <facts>
            moves    <who feels what, in words not numbers>
            cues     <named sensory beats>
            prose    <written out>

what it is for     <one line: what this exists to make possible downstream>
what it costs      <what leaves the deck once this can happen, or what the
                    player gives up by reaching it>
```

Use the last two lines seriously. A situation that costs nothing is usually one
that has not been thought about.

## 7. The rules, as a checklist

- Every gate has a **ceiling**. Name what stops being true.
- **Never name another situation.** Declare needs as facts.
- Every fact has **two or more origins**.
- Every situation has an outcome reachable with the player doing **nothing at
  all**, and it has to be worth reading. The well must be able to be just a well.
- **No new verbs.** Press and hold are the entire input surface, forever.
  Affordances change what they *mean* here, never how many there are.
- Registers only on situations carrying plot weight. A register is a tell on the
  main story; a tell on a throwaway scene is a lie.
- Every situation names **where it can be cut short**, and the line that ends it
  there. A scene that cannot survive being cut is a scene that will break at high
  dread.
- Beliefs are **overheard**, never summarised, never shown.
- Sound before sight. The player is at the bottom of a hole; almost everything
  arrives as noise first.
- **Do not write a mystery that requires seeing three particular scenes.** The
  deal is random and the machine cannot promise anyone a deduction. The truth of
  the death stays partly ambiguous — that is a structural requirement, not
  coyness.
- Nothing warm survives contact with the well without cost.
- The word *dead* never appears.

## 8. What is not yours to decide

Numbers and tuning; how the machine picks; anything about the interface, the
camera, or audio implementation. If a piece of writing needs a rule that does not
exist yet, **say so in a list at the end of your output rather than inventing
it** — those are the interesting findings and I want them separated from the
prose.

If something in this brief contradicts itself, say which two things and which way
you resolved it. Ask questions in a batch, not one at a time.
